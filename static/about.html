About the model

What it predicts, and where it breaks

A gradient boosting classifier that estimates the probability a diabetic inpatient is readmitted within 30 days of discharge. This page is the short version of the model card: what went in, how well it scores, and the groups where it measurably fails.

Model details

  • Model: scikit-learn HistGradientBoostingClassifier — 300 iterations, learning rate 0.05, 31 max leaf nodes, L2 = 1.0, early stopping — inside a Pipeline with median imputation and scaling for numerics, and constant-MISSING imputation with one-hot encoding (min frequency 20) for categoricals.
  • Inputs: 8 numeric and 14 categorical fields describing a single hospital encounter. GET /schema lists them with their valid values.
  • Output: a probability of readmission within 30 days, plus a risk band (low < 15% ≤ elevated < 25% ≤ high), any subgroup caveats that apply, and the fields driving that particular score.
  • Version: trained 2026-09-08 on the patient-grouped 80% split (79k encounters), scikit-learn 1.9.0. The remaining 20% is held out; a 1,000-row sample ships with the app for the live calibration check on the result page.

Intended use

The primary purpose is to demonstrate an end-to-end tabular ML workflow on real de-identified clinical data — leakage-safe validation, calibration, error analysis, subgroup reporting, serving, and explanation. The intended audience is people evaluating the engineering and the analysis, through this demo, the API, or the repository.

Out of scope: any clinical, operational, or insurance decision about a real patient. The data are from US hospitals between 1999 and 2008; care patterns, coding practice, and populations have all changed since. The model has never been externally validated, and it underperforms in the groups listed below.

The data

UCI Diabetes 130-US Hospitals for Years 1999–2008 (Clore et al., 2014; CC BY 4.0): 101,766 encounters from 71,518 unique patients across 130 US hospitals. After the exclusions described below, 99,343 encounters remain. The positive rate — readmission within 30 days — is 11.4%.

Three decisions shaped what the model sees:

  • weight is 97% missing and is dropped outright.
  • A1Cresult and max_glu_serum are 85–95% missing, but a test not being ordered is itself a signal. They keep "not tested" as its own category rather than being imputed away.
  • diag_1, diag_2 and diag_3 hold roughly 700 distinct ICD-9 codes each. These are grouped into nine clinical categories — circulatory, respiratory, diabetes, and so on — rather than one-hot encoded raw.

Why death and hospice encounters are excluded

2,423 encounters (2.4% of the data) end in death or a transfer to hospice — discharge disposition codes 11, 13, 14, 19, 20 and 21. These patients cannot be readmitted. Their recorded 30-day readmission rate is 1.8%, against 11.4% for everyone else, and that gap is an artifact of mortality rather than of risk.

Left in, they hand the model a shortcut: learning that discharge code 11 means no readmission is correct, and clinically useless. It would inflate every headline metric while teaching the model nothing about the patients a discharge planner actually needs to identify. So they are removed before training, and the model is only ever asked about patients who could come back.

How well it scores

5-fold GroupKFold cross-validation, grouped by patient_nbr:

ModelPR-AUCvs. base rate
Dummy (predicts the base rate)0.1139 ± 0.0019—
Logistic regression0.2153 ± 0.0048+89%
Gradient boosting0.2306 ± 0.0040+102%

Boosting beats logistic regression in 5 of 5 folds (mean gain 0.0154, worst fold +0.0087), so the improvement survives resampling rather than being an artifact of one split. Patients in the top predicted-risk decile readmit at 27.8% against 4.0% in the bottom decile — a 6.9× lift. Probabilities are calibrated, so a score can be read as an actual risk and not merely a ranking.

ROC-AUC on the held-out test set is 0.669, much higher than the PR-AUC because the large negative class flatters the false-positive rate. At an 11.4% base rate, accuracy and ROC-AUC flatter any model — predicting "no" for every patient scores 88.6% accuracy — so PR-AUC is the metric reported throughout.

Where it fails

Subgroup performance on the held-out test set:

GroupnPR-AUCNote
Age 90–1005470.194 (ROC 0.540) Near chance, against roughly 0.65 ROC-AUC for other age bands
African American3,7070.190 vs 0.238 for Caucasian patients (n = 14,886) at similar base rates
Race unrecorded4180.168 Weakest group measured, but on a small sample

The 90–100 result is not a base-rate artifact: that group has the highest readmission rate of any age band, at 12.8%. The model is weakest exactly where the patients most likely to return are. Plausibly the available features don't capture what drives readmission in the very elderly — frailty, social support, care setting.

The race gap is measured on samples large enough for the estimate to be stable: base rates are close (10.7% vs 11.5%), so this is a difference in how well the model ranks, not a difference in who gets readmitted.

Subgroups below roughly 500 encounters (about 40 positives) give unstable PR-AUC estimates. That covers the unrecorded-race group and puts the 90–100 band close to the line, so both should be read as indicative rather than settled. Small subgroups that score well — ages 20–30, PR-AUC 0.537 on n=388 — are not reported as strengths, for exactly the same reason.

This model should not be deployed for the 90+ population, and the performance gap across race groups would need to be addressed before any clinical use.

Fairness and ethical considerations

  • Race, gender and age are model inputs. They are kept deliberately: the analysis reports performance by group and the service warns at the point of use. Removing them would hide the gap, not close it — and a deployable model would need that gap addressed first.
  • Known limitations travel with the prediction. If a request falls into an affected subgroup, POST /predict returns the caveat in its response and the result page shows it in red above the score. A consumer is warned where they act, rather than being expected to have read the documentation.
  • Prior hospital utilisation dominates the signal. A model like this can reinforce existing patterns of care if it is used to allocate resources without oversight.
  • Do not use it for the 90+ population at all.
  • Retraining on current data and external validation would both be required before any real-world use.
